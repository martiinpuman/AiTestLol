# Security review — ADR-0029 and the nine identity/authorization bootstrap rows

- **Reviewer:** security-reviewer (not the author)
- **Date:** 2026-09-11
- **Branch:** `task/ARCH-IDENTITY`
- **Scope:** `docs/decisions/ADR-0029-…md`; `docs/architecture/solution-layout.md` §6.2/§6.3; the status-line amendments to ADR-0007, ADR-0009, ADR-0010, ADR-0012; read against ADR-0005 rule 2, ADR-0007 §3/§4/§9/§10, ADR-0012 §3, ADR-0028, SPEC-001, SPEC-002, `testing-strategy.md` §5.6.
- **Tier:** Full. Design review — nothing is implemented.
- **Verdict:** **CHANGES_REQUESTED** — 2 blockers, 9 high.

## 1. What I attacked, and what happened

This is a design, so "executed" means reading the design as an implementer would and then reading the
framework it mandates. Where I assert a framework behaviour (cookie renewal, `SecurityStampValidator`,
`RevalidatingServerAuthenticationStateProvider`) I name the mechanism so a developer can check it in an
hour, not argue about it.

**The central claim — a cookie minted for tenant A is authentic at tenant B, and `tid` is all that stands
between it and tenant B's data.** The claim is correct and the ADR is right to make it the load-bearing
paragraph. The replay test in §4 and B-18.5 AC-4 (shared key ring, asserted shared; counted-zero
connections; the `/t/{b}/` single-host variant) is the best-specified control in the document and I could
not weaken it *as a test*. What I could do is get round the thing it tests.

| Attack | Result |
|---|---|
| Replay tenant A's cookie at tenant B's host | Refused by checkpoint 1 **if** the middleware runs after `UseAuthentication` and the path is not exempt. Neither is pinned — **H-4** |
| Never replay anything: get a `tid` minted *for* tenant B instead | **Works.** `ITenantMembership` is cached under `c:member:{userId}` — no tenant in the key. Sign in to my own tenant, then sign in at the victim's host within 60 s — **B-1** |
| Reach tenant data on a path checkpoint 1 does not cover (`/_blazor`, static, health, `/sign-in`) | Undecided by the design. The ADR names no exemption list and no pipeline position; under path-based tenancy `/_blazor` resolves to no tenant at all — **H-4** |
| Open a `TenantScope` whose tenant differs from the principal's `tid` after middleware | Nothing stops it. The evaluator receives both halves and compares neither — **H-1** |
| Widen a `Granted(CompanyScope)` to another company inside the tenant | Nothing in the design says how `CompanyScope` reaches the EF filter — **H-2** |
| Get a permission nobody should hold | The per-tenant seeder grants the administrator *every permission in the catalogue* — a wildcard by another name, and it contradicts the ADR's own no-superuser test — **H-3** |
| Keep using a session after revocation / suspension / `SchemaBlocked` | 30 minutes on the circuit path; **unbounded (12 h)** on the plain HTTP path, which has no revalidation at all — **H-5** |
| Make the framework re-mint or drop `tid` without a re-authentication | `SecurityStampValidator` replaces the principal with a freshly built one. Both outcomes are bad — **H-6** |
| Keep a session alive after the user signs out | Live circuits survive sign-out; nothing rotates the security stamp — **H-7** |
| Take over an existing Aurora account through provisioning | **Works.** An invitation is a global password-set capability and the token is returned to the *caller* — **B-2** |
| Deny sign-in to the whole fleet, unauthenticated | Every failed sign-in writes to the shared catalog, and a failed write fails the sign-in; all rate limiting is deferred — **H-8** |
| Forge the identity that a resumed circuit runs as | Reconnect compares the tenant only, not the subject or the stamp; where the pinned tenant lives during persistence is unspecified — **H-9** |
| Append attacker-chosen rows to a tenant's hash-chained audit log | Boundary is drawn correctly (§7) and I could not get round it — **except** via B-1, which forges the "proof" the boundary depends on |
| Enumerate the customer list | `403` for "exists but not yours" vs `404` for "no such tenant" is an oracle, contradicting §4.2 — **M-1** |

### Things I attacked and found sound — say so

- **Refusing rather than re-minting on mismatch** (§4, options table row 1). Correct, and the rejected
  alternative ("re-mint if the user is a member") is rejected for the right reason.
- **`tid` as a constraint and never a source**, and absence-is-mismatch. Correct. This is the single
  most important sentence in the ADR and it is the right way round.
- **Not reusing `TenantRoutingViolationException`** (§4.5). The reasoning is right and I tried to break
  it: §4.3's exception marks the tenant `SchemaBlocked`, so if a claim mismatch threw it, any stale
  cookie would be a one-request denial-of-service against a tenant, and a cookie is trivially obtained
  (your own, after your membership is revoked). Keeping the two apart is correct. See M-2/L-1 for the
  two small problems the split introduces — neither undoes the decision.
- **Not deleting the cookie on refusal** (§4.3). Correct: deleting it converts a crafted link into a
  logout oracle/DoS against the victim and gains nothing against the holder.
- **Permissions never in the cookie or a token** (§6), and the token-intersection rule. Correct, and
  S6 makes it a reviewed edit rather than a factory nobody reads.
- **`CompanyScope.Of` throwing on empty** (§5). Correct and structural — the empty set cannot be
  constructed, so it cannot decay to `WHERE 1=1`. Good shape. Its weakness is transport, not
  construction (H-2).
- **no-decision → `500` / store-unavailable → `503`, never `Denied`** (§5). Correct, and the operational
  argument (two alarms for two causes) is right. One enumerated row does manufacture a `Denied` from an
  error state — M-3.
- **The audit split** (§7). The boundary is in the right place: the rule is "unproven caller ⇒ platform
  side", not "unauthenticated caller ⇒ platform side", and that is the stronger of the two. I checked
  the tenant-side rows for attacker-chosen content and found none — permission and request-type are
  code constants, the actor is proven. The volume, not the content, is the problem (M-6).
- **"A sign-in that cannot be recorded does not happen"** is the right default in isolation. Combined
  with deferring rate limiting it is H-8; the fix is to the rate limiting, not to this rule.

## 2. Blockers (critical)

### B-1 — The Tenant Membership cache key has no tenant in it: a `tid` for a tenant you do not belong to

- **Where:** `docs/architecture/solution-layout.md:343` (B-18.2 criterion 5), `docs/decisions/ADR-0012-caching.md:3`.
- **ASVS:** V4.1.3 (least privilege enforced server-side), V8.1 (cached data segregation).

B-18.2 criterion 5 says `ITenantMembership` answers *"is this user an active member of this tenant"* and is
**"cached 60 s under `c:member:{userId}`"**. The question is asked per `(user, tenant)`; the key names only
the user. The obvious implementation — the one a developer transcribing this row will write — is:

```
var active = await cache.GetOrCreateAsync($"c:member:{userId}",
                  ct => store.IsActiveMemberAsync(userId, tenantId, ct));   // tenantId is NOT in the key
```

**The attack.** I am a legitimate user of any Aurora tenant — my own trial tenant, or my employer's.

1. Sign in at `mine.aurora.example`. The membership check for `(me, mine)` returns `Active` and caches
   `true` under `c:member:{me}`.
2. Within 60 s, POST `/sign-in` at `globex.aurora.example` with my own correct password.
3. B-18.5 criterion 3's precondition *"the Tenant Membership for the host-resolved tenant is `Active`"* is
   evaluated against the cache, hits my entry, and returns `Active`.
4. A `tid` for Globex is **minted**, by the one legitimate mint site, for a tenant I have never been a
   member of. Checkpoint 1 then passes forever after, because the claim and the host now agree. Checkpoint 3
   re-asks the same cached question and also passes.

This is not a cookie replay — it defeats the mint preconditions, which is why none of the §4 replay tests
see it. Blast radius is bounded by B-17.2 (no role assignments in Globex ⇒ every guarded command denied),
but bounded is not zero:

- Every ungated read in the shell — tenant display name, company picker, installed packages, feature
  flags, localization overrides — runs inside a valid Globex `TenantScope`.
- A **successful sign-in writes a tenant-side `audit.audit_event`** (§7 row 1). So the one thing §7 exists
  to prevent — an unproven caller appending attacker-chosen rows to a customer's hash-chained log —
  happens, and it happens with the chain's own proof-of-membership forged.
- It converts every subsequent defence into the last line of defence.

**Why no acceptance criterion catches it.** B-18.2 criterion 5's only cache test is *"revoke a membership
and assert the next call does not serve the cached row"* — which passes under the vulnerable
implementation, because revocation invalidates `c:member:{me}` regardless of tenant. The criterion cannot
fail the flaw (`CLAUDE.md` self-check 2/3).

**Two further defects in the same line.** (a) ADR-0012 rule 1 requires every cache key to be produced by
`TenantCacheKey.For(scope, …)` with a `t:{tenantId}:` prefix, asserted by a fitness test; a raw
`$"c:member:{userId}"` violates that rule as written and there is no catalog-key helper defined anywhere.
(b) Both `solution-layout.md:343` and `ADR-0012:3` cite **ADR-0029 §4** as the definition of this key.
ADR-0029 §4 contains no cache key and the string `c:member` does not appear anywhere in ADR-0029. A key
that governs a tenancy boundary was invented in a task row and back-cited to an ADR section that does not
contain it.

**Fix (design-level, one paragraph in ADR-0029 §4):**
- The membership entry is keyed by **both** identifiers — `c:member:{tenantId}:{userId}` — or the cached
  value is the user's **complete** membership map (`tenantId -> state`) and the tenant lookup happens
  *after* the cache read, never in the factory. State which; do not leave it to the row.
- Add a criterion to B-18.2 that can fail this: *user U is an active member of tenant A and not of
  tenant B; ask `ITenantMembership` for (U, A) then immediately for (U, B) with no time advanced; the
  second answer is `false`.* That test is three lines and it is the whole finding.
- Add the mint-level equivalent to B-18.5: *sign in successfully at A, then attempt sign-in at B with
  correct credentials and no membership in B, within the cache TTL; assert refusal and assert zero rows
  in B's `audit.audit_event`.*
- Define the catalog-side key helper (`CatalogCacheKey.For(...)`) so ADR-0012 rule 1's fitness test has a
  legal form to accept, rather than an exception someone adds under deadline.

### B-2 — An invitation is a global password-set capability, and the token is handed to the caller

- **Where:** `docs/architecture/solution-layout.md:346-355` (B-18.3, criteria 1, 2, 4), SPEC-001 BR-2/BR-3,
  ADR-0007 §9.2 (`catalog.identity_user.email_normalized unique`), ADR-0010 rule 8.
- **ASVS:** V2.3 (credential recovery), V4.3 (administrative interfaces).

Credentials are **global**: one `catalog.identity_user` per email across the whole platform, one
`catalog.identity_credential` behind it (ADR-0029 §2). Email is globally unique. B-18.3 criterion 4 says
redemption *"sets the password through `UserManager`"* — i.e. it sets that person's **platform-wide**
password. B-18.3 criterion 2 says the token *"is returned to the caller exactly once"* — and the caller is
the provisioning saga's initiator, not the invited person. SPEC-001 BR-2 lets the provisioning request
name **any email address**.

**The attack.** An operator (or the onboarding harness, or whatever calls provisioning when self-service
arrives — SPEC-001's non-goals defer the page, not the capability) provisions a throwaway tenant naming
`cfo@victim-customer.example` as first administrator. `catalog.identity_user` already has that row, because
email is globally unique. The saga adopts it, issues an invitation, and the API response hands the caller a
token that sets that person's password. Redeem it, and you hold the victim's platform identity — including
**every tenant they are a member of**, with their existing memberships and their existing administrator
Role Assignments.

This is precisely the path ADR-0010 rule 8 declares impossible: *"Aurora staff never hold standing
permissions in a tenant … [time-boxed, reason-coded operator access] is the only path by which a non-member
reaches tenant data."* It also makes SPEC-001 BR-3's *"the requirement is only that the administrator can
reach a real sign-in, never that Aurora staff hand them a shared or guessed password"* false by
construction — staff hold the token that sets it. Nothing lands in `catalog.operator_audit_event` as support
access, and the victim's tenant sees a normal sign-in by its own CFO.

There is a second, unowned half: **no row decides what provisioning does when the administrator email
already exists.** B-18.3 takes a `UserId`, so someone upstream resolved the email to a user. Adopt, refuse
or create-new-and-collide is a security decision with no owner — and "adopt" is the default that makes the
attack work.

**Fix (in ADR-0029 §7 or a new §, and in B-18.3):**
1. An invitation may only be **issued** against an identity that has **no credential and no membership in
   any other tenant**. If the email already belongs to an established user, provisioning creates a
   *Tenant Membership* for them and sends a *join* notification — it never gets a password-set token.
   Redeeming a join requires authenticating as that user.
2. The token is **delivered to the invited address**, never returned in the API response to the caller.
   If bootstrap has no mail transport, return an opaque handle and log the delivery intent — do not return
   the secret. (B-18.3 criterion 5 already forbids the token in logs and metrics; returning it to a
   third party is the same disclosure with a nicer wrapper.)
3. Redemption verifies the invitation's `tenant_id` equals the **host/path-resolved** tenant of the
   redeem request, per §4's "never from a form field, a query string, a previous cookie or a header".
   B-18.3 is silent on where the redeem endpoint lives and which tenant it resolves; say it.
4. Name the owner of find-or-create on `catalog.identity_user` and its refusal rule. Add a criterion:
   *provisioning a tenant naming an email that already has a credential does not produce a password-set
   token* — a test that fails today.

## 3. High

### H-1 — The evaluator holds both halves of the tenancy proof and compares neither (the fourth no-decision case)

**Where:** ADR-0029 §5 signature (line 97-99) and outcome table (103-113); B-17.2 (`solution-layout.md:300-309`).

```
EvaluateAsync(ClaimsPrincipal principal, TenantScope scope, Permission permission, CompanyId? target, ct)
```

`principal` carries `tid`. `scope` carries `TenantId`. The table enumerates seven situations and **none of
them is "these two disagree"**. Today the agreement is a *convention*: "the middleware ran first". That
convention does not hold for:

- any request on a path checkpoint 1 does not cover (H-4);
- a background job or outbox consumer, which opens a scope from a `TenantId` argument (ADR-0007 §10) with
  no middleware anywhere, and which under ADR-0010 rule 9 may carry "the permission context of whoever
  caused them" — i.e. a principal from somewhere else entirely;
- any future in-process fan-out or admin path that opens a second scope inside one request.

Consequence with real teeth: pass a multi-tenant user's principal (`tid = A`) with tenant **B**'s scope and
the evaluator reads **B's** `access.role_assignment` for that `user_id`. For the external accountant —
ADR-0009's motivating case — that is a **grant**, authorizing an action in B against a request routed for A.
Not exotic: it is one mis-wired job or one `OpenAsync` in the wrong branch.

This is exactly the shape the brief asks for: the design's whole tenancy strategy is "make the unsafe thing
impossible to express", and here the one method that can see both halves of the proof declines to check them.

**Fix:** add an eighth row: *principal's `tid` absent, unparsable, or `!= scope.TenantId`* → **no decision**,
throws (`500`), counter `authz_tenant_mismatch_total`. Never `Denied` — it is a defect, not a user error.
Add it as a B-17.2 criterion with a test that constructs the mismatch directly, not through HTTP.

### H-2 — `CompanyScope` is constructed safely and then transported by unspecified means

**Where:** ADR-0029 §5 (lines 117-123); ADR-0010 rules 4/6; B-17.2 criteria 1-2; B-15.1 (SPEC-002 BR-4).

`CompanyScope.Of` cannot be empty — good. But `CompanyScope` is *"the parameter of ADR-0010 rule 6's EF
global query filter"*, and **nothing says how it gets from the `PermissionDecision` to the `DbContext`**.
A global query filter is configured per context; the decision is produced per command, after the context
factory is usually already in hand. The two implementations a developer will reach for are:

1. an ambient `ICompanyScopeAccessor` — the exact failure ADR-0007 §3.3 bans for tenants: correct in
   development, correct on first render, stale or interleaved inside a live circuit where two component
   events run concurrently; or
2. nothing at all — the handler receives `Granted(scope)` and writes the `Where` by hand, i.e. rule 6's
   "filter, not post-filter" becomes a code-review item.

Either way the **company** boundary — half of ADR-0010's "per tenant *and* per company" requirement, and
the one horizontal-escalation boundary inside a tenant — is a convention, while the tenant boundary is a
mechanism. `WHERE 1=1` cannot be *constructed*; it can still be *forgotten*.

**Fix:** make it structural in the same shape as `TenantScope`: `CompanyScope` is a **required parameter**
of the context factory (`CreateAsync(TenantScope, CompanyScope, ct)`) or of the application-service method
signature (ADR-0007 §4.5's pattern), so a filtered context cannot be obtained without one. Add a fitness
rule: no `[RequiresPermission]`-guarded handler obtains a context for a company-scoped entity without a
`CompanyScope`. Add a B-17.2 or B-17.3 criterion: *a user granted company A only, issuing the tenant-wide
list, receives A's rows and only A's* — asserted against the generated SQL or a second company's rows,
not against a hand-written `Where`.

### H-3 — "Grant the administrator every permission in the catalogue" is a wildcard, and it contradicts the ADR's own no-superuser test

**Where:** B-17.1 criterion 5 (`solution-layout.md:295`) vs ADR-0029 §5 (line 109 and line 125) and
B-17.2 criterion 3 (`solution-layout.md:306`).

- B-17.1 AC-5: the seeder *"grants the built-in administrator role **every permission in the catalogue
  after the upsert**"*.
- ADR-0029 §5: *"Permission in the catalogue held by **no** role — including the built-in administrator
  role — `Denied`, for everyone"*, proven by *"declare a test-only permission, seed it into the catalogue,
  grant it to nobody, assert the administrator is denied."*

A permission that is in the catalogue and granted to nobody **cannot exist** if the seeder grants the
administrator everything in the catalogue. B-17.1 AC-5 and B-17.2 AC-3 cannot both pass against one
implementation; one of them will be made to pass by bypassing the seeder in the fixture, and the
no-superuser test — the ADR's evidence that there is no hidden branch — will then be evidence of nothing.

The security content, not just the contradiction: "every permission in the catalogue, automatically, at
every migration" **is** a wildcard, described in the same paragraph that boasts *"without a wildcard"*. It
means every permission any module ever declares is held by every tenant's administrator the moment the
`access` schema migrates — including permissions intended for platform or operator use. SPEC-001 says the
provisioning command *"must declare its required permission explicitly so fitness rule S1 covers it"*; the
natural way to do that is a `Permission` constant, and the seeder will then hand it to every tenant
administrator in the fleet. ADR-0010 rule 8's separation of operator capability from tenant permission
survives only as long as nobody declares an operator capability as a `Permission`.

**Fix:** the built-in administrator's permission set is an **explicit, committed list** — an
approved-permissions snapshot in the shape of S6's approved-claims file. New permissions join it by a
reviewed edit; the seeder grants that list, not "everything". This keeps ADR-0029 §5's stated benefit
(SPEC-002's `organization.company.manage` reaches an administrator provisioned earlier — because the
reviewed edit adds it), makes the no-superuser test meaningful, and makes "a permission no role holds"
constructible. Add the counterpart rule: a `Permission` constant may not be declared for a platform or
operator capability; those live outside the tenant catalogue (ADR-0010 rule 8), asserted by a namespace
rule.

### H-4 — Checkpoint 1's position and exemptions are unspecified, and both ways of getting them wrong are silent

**Where:** ADR-0029 §4 checkpoint table (line 69) and line 73; B-18.4 (`solution-layout.md:357-366`).

The check is specified relative to *tenant resolution* ("after host/path resolution, before
`ITenantScopeFactory.OpenAsync`") and not at all relative to the ASP.NET Core pipeline. Two gaps:

1. **Ordering against `UseAuthentication`.** If the resolution middleware is registered before
   authentication, `HttpContext.User` is anonymous for every request, "carrying an authenticated
   principal" is never true, and the control evaluates to a no-op — a security control that silently does
   nothing, which is the exact failure `CLAUDE.md`'s self-check 2 was written about. The developer who
   registers it before authentication will see nothing wrong: the app works.
2. **Exempt paths.** §4 says a request whose host/path resolves to no tenant is refused `404`. Under
   path-based tenancy (`/t/{key}`, one host) that is **every request that is not under `/t/{key}`**:
   `/_blazor` (the SignalR negotiate and websocket, mapped at the application root), static assets,
   health endpoints, `/sign-in`, `/sign-out`, the deferred tenant picker. The design names no exemption
   list, so one will be invented by whoever implements B-18.4, unreviewed — and whatever is on it is a
   path where an authenticated principal reaches the application with **no** cross-check. If `/_blazor`
   lands on it (it must, or Blazor does not work), the single longest-lived and most valuable path in the
   product is exempt from checkpoint 1 and rests entirely on checkpoint 2, whose own input is unspecified
   (H-9).

There is also a self-inflicted wedge: a user holding tenant A's cookie is `403`'d **before** reaching
`/sign-out` at any other tenant's host, and before reaching `/sign-in` there. If tenant A is suspended or
deleted, there is no in-product way to get rid of the cookie; "clear your browser cookies" is the recovery
procedure. That will produce pressure to exempt exactly the paths that matter.

**Fix:**
- Pin the position: *the cross-check runs inside the authentication result path* — implement it in
  `CookieAuthenticationOptions.Events.OnValidatePrincipal` (which has the full `HttpContext`, so host and
  path are available), **not** as a separately ordered middleware. Then it cannot be ordered wrong and it
  cannot be path-exempted by accident, because it runs wherever the cookie is read. Keep the refusal
  itself in the pipeline (`OnValidatePrincipal` must not call `SignOutAsync` — §4.3 forbids deleting the
  cookie).
- If it stays a middleware, state the registration order explicitly (`UseAuthentication` →
  cross-check → `UseAuthorization` → endpoints) and add a B-18.4 criterion that **fails when the order is
  wrong**: register it before authentication in a deliberately-violating fixture and assert the replay
  test goes red.
- Name the exempt set explicitly in the ADR (`/sign-in`, `/sign-out`, `/_blazor`, `/health*`, static
  assets), with the justification per entry, on the same reviewed-file footing as S2's `[AllowAnonymous]`
  allow-list — and state for each why an unchecked principal there is harmless.
- State what `/_blazor` resolves to under path-based tenancy and how checkpoint 2 gets its tenant (H-9).

### H-5 — Revocation, suspension and `SchemaBlocked` are unenforced for up to 30 minutes on the circuit path and unbounded on the HTTP path

**Where:** ADR-0029 §4 checkpoints 1/3 (lines 69, 71), §6 (line 129), Consequences (line 173);
B-18.6 criterion 2.

The ADR's staleness argument is *"a revoked permission stops working within 60 s"*. That argument is about
**permissions** and it holds. It does **not** carry to the other four things:

| Changed fact | Re-checked on the circuit path | Re-checked on a plain HTTP (API/non-circuit) request |
|---|---|---|
| Permission / role assignment | ≤ 60 s (cache TTL) | ≤ 60 s |
| Tenant Membership revoked | ≤ 30 min (checkpoint 3) | **never** |
| `catalog.tenant.state` → `Suspended`/`SchemaBlocked`/`PendingDeletion` | ≤ 30 min | **never** |
| Security stamp (password change, forced sign-out, user disabled) | ≤ 30 min | **never** |
| `catalog.identity_user.status` | never | never |

Checkpoint 1 compares `tid` and **nothing else**. So for the REST path that B-15.2 builds — cookie
authenticated, no circuit — a revoked member, a disabled user or a suspended tenant continues to work for
the full 12-hour absolute cookie lifetime. Permission evaluation does not save it: the user still holds
their Role Assignments in that tenant; it is the *membership*, not the permission, that was removed.

The `SchemaBlocked` case is worse than a policy lapse. `SchemaBlocked` means a migration failed and the
tenant was quarantined (B-08). A live circuit and any cookie-authenticated client keep **writing** to a
half-migrated database for up to 30 minutes, or indefinitely. `Exporting`/`PendingDeletion` (offboarding)
has the same shape: writes during an export make the export wrong.

**Fix — one choke point, not five checks:** `ITenantScopeFactory.OpenAsync` already re-reads the routing
row (60 s cache, *invalidated on tenant state change*, ADR-0007 §3.5, and ADR-0012 rule 6 evicts on
suspend) and already runs the §7.5 skew gate (B-08.3). Make it **refuse to open a scope for a tenant whose
state is not in a named allow-list per `TenantAccessReason`** — `Request` requires `Active`; `Migration`
may open `SchemaBlocked`; `Provisioning` may open `Provisioning`; and so on. That is a mechanism, it is
one place, it bounds tenant-state staleness to the routing cache's 60 s for *every* path including jobs
and the outbox, and it needs no new check anywhere else. Add the membership re-check to checkpoint 1 as
well (it is the same 60 s cached read the sign-in already does — once B-1 is fixed), or state explicitly
in the ADR that the HTTP path's revocation bound is 12 hours and have someone sign that off.

### H-6 — ASP.NET Core's own revalidator amends the principal in place; the "one mint site" invariant does not survive contact with the framework

**Where:** ADR-0029 §4 (line 63: *"a **new** `tid` is only ever produced by a full re-authentication …
never by amending a principal in place"*), §2 (line 53), §8 rule S7; B-18.2 criterion 1.

`SecurityStampValidator<TUser>` — the standard way to make cookie sessions honour a security stamp, and the
thing you reach for the moment you notice H-5 — does this on every validation interval:

```csharp
var newPrincipal = await SignInManager.CreateUserPrincipalAsync(user);
// Options.OnRefreshingPrincipal gives you a chance to carry claims over
context.ReplacePrincipal(newPrincipal);
context.ShouldRenew = true;
```

It **rebuilds the principal from the user store and replaces it**, re-issuing the cookie. `tid` is not a
store claim, so the claims factory will not produce it. Two outcomes, both bad, both silent until they are
loud:

- **Claim dropped:** every session in the fleet loses `tid` at the same interval, and §4's
  absence-is-mismatch turns that into a simultaneous `403 tenant-mismatch` for every user. Fail-closed, so
  not a breach — but indistinguishable, in the metric the ADR proposes to watch, from an attack, and the
  `Revisit when` trigger ("`403`s dominated by legitimate navigation") will read it as a routing problem.
- **Claim carried over via `OnRefreshingPrincipal`** (the documented fix): that copy site now touches
  `AuroraClaimTypes.TenantId`, fails fitness rule S7, gets added to S7's allow-list under deadline — and
  the system now has a place that writes `tid` into a principal **outside a re-authentication**, with
  none of §4's mint preconditions (no membership re-check, no tenant-state check, no host resolution).
  The invariant the whole design rests on is now three sites and a fitness-rule exception.

Related weakness in the S7 mechanism itself: it is a rule about references to a **constant**. A developer
who writes `new Claim("tid", …)` — or any code path that constructs a `ClaimsIdentity` from a stored claim
list — never touches the constant and never trips the rule. A rule named "`tid` is minted in one place"
must inspect the string literal and the identity-construction sites too, or be renamed to what it actually
checks (`CLAUDE.md` self-check 1). Note also that `tkey` — the claim that names the **strategy-2 routing
key** — is in the minted claim set (§6 line 133) and is guarded by **nothing**: S7 covers `TenantId` only.

**Fix:** decide, in the ADR: either (a) `SecurityStampValidator` is not used on the cookie path and the
HTTP-path revocation story is solved another way (H-5), or (b) it is used, `OnRefreshingPrincipal`
re-runs the §4 mint preconditions (membership `Active`, tenant `Active`, host-resolved tenant equals the
old `tid`) and **rejects** rather than copying when any fails — in which case it is a fourth checkpoint and
belongs in §4's table, not in an allow-list exception. Extend S7 to the literal, to `ClaimsIdentity`
construction, and to `TenantKey`.

### H-7 — Sign-out does not end the session it signs out of

**Where:** ADR-0029 §7 (line 142), B-18.5 criterion 5, B-18.6 criterion 2. **ASVS V3.3.1.**

Sign-out is specified as an audit event and a cookie deletion. Nothing in the design:

- rotates the security stamp, so the 30-minute revalidation tick has nothing to notice;
- terminates the user's **live circuits**. A Blazor Server circuit holds its own principal and issues no
  HTTP requests; deleting a cookie in one tab does not touch the circuit in another. On a shared or
  kiosk workstation — an ERP's normal habitat, warehouse and shop floor — the next person at the keyboard
  finds a fully authenticated session for up to 30 minutes;
- terminates other sessions after a **password change** (the same mechanism, the same gap).

There is a second-order version: `RevalidatingServerAuthenticationStateProvider`'s failure path calls
`ForceSignOut()`, which sets an **anonymous authentication state and leaves the circuit connected**. The
already-rendered tenant data stays on screen and in circuit memory until a navigation. B-18.6 criterion 1
requires abort-not-downgrade for the `tid` mismatch at creation/reconnect; criterion 3 only requires the
principal to be "invalidated" on the revalidation tick, which stock behaviour satisfies while leaving the
data visible.

**Fix:** sign-out calls `UserManager.UpdateSecurityStampAsync` (this is what makes revalidation see it),
and the revalidation failure path forces a navigation to the sign-in page rather than an anonymous
render. Add B-18.5 criteria: *after sign-out, a circuit opened before the sign-out is invalidated at the
next revalidation tick*, and *a password change invalidates other sessions*. Both are `FakeTimeProvider`
tests in the shape B-18.6 criterion 3 already uses.

### H-8 — Unauthenticated writes into the shared catalog, plus fail-closed on that write, is a fleet-wide sign-in outage

**Where:** ADR-0029 §7 (line 148, line 150), §9 (line 164: per-IP/per-host rate limiting deferred);
ADR-0007 §9.4 (the catalog is a single point of failure and is treated as one).

Three individually reasonable decisions compose badly:

1. every **failed** sign-in writes a row to `catalog.authentication_event` — the shared catalog;
2. *"A sign-in that cannot be recorded does not happen: if the `catalog.authentication_event` write fails,
   the sign-in fails"*;
3. all rate limiting is deferred; bootstrap relies on **per-account lockout**, which does nothing against
   an attacker who varies the email address.

An unauthenticated attacker posting `/sign-in` with random addresses writes unbounded rows into the one
database every tenant in the fleet shares, with attacker-influenced `host` and `detail` values, at whatever
rate they can manage. Password verification costs a deliberate KDF on the server, so the CPU asymmetry runs
against us too. When the catalog degrades — table bloat, autovacuum, connection exhaustion, disk — rule (2)
converts that degradation into **"nobody in any tenant can sign in"**. Rule (2) is right in isolation;
combined with (3) it hands an unauthenticated attacker the fleet's sign-in availability.

There is no retention rule for this table anywhere (ADR-0018's retention is tenant-side), and it is
append-only, so nothing removes the rows.

**Fix — do not defer rate limiting past B-18.5:** add per-IP and per-(email-hash) fixed-window limiting on
`/sign-in` and on invitation redeem (`Microsoft.AspNetCore.RateLimiting`, in-box, zero new dependencies) as
a criterion of B-18.5, with a test that the limiter rejects before the credential check and before the
catalog write. Additionally: record *failed* attempts at a bounded granularity (one row per
(source, email-hash, minute) with a counter, rather than one row per attempt), and give
`catalog.authentication_event` a stated retention window and the platform job that enforces it.

### H-9 — Circuit reconnect proves the tenant and not the person; the pinned tenant's transport is unspecified

**Where:** ADR-0029 §4 checkpoint 2 (line 70); B-18.6 criteria 1, 5, 6.

Checkpoint 2 compares *"`tid` on the principal handed to the circuit vs the tenant the creating request
resolved"*. Two unanswered questions, both load-bearing:

1. **What is "the tenant the creating request resolved" for a *reconnect*, and where did it live in
   between?** .NET 10 persisted circuit state is exactly the mechanism the ADR cites. If the pinned tenant
   is carried in persisted component state or any client round-tripped payload, then the cross-check
   compares a cookie claim against a value the client can set, and the control degrades to "the attacker
   must set both consistently" — no control at all. B-18.6 criterion 5 gestures at this (T5/T6 extended to
   the pinned `TenantId`, "not in a serialized payload"), which is the right instinct but leaves the
   positive requirement unstated: **where does the pinned tenant live for a persisted circuit?** Say
   "server-side only, keyed by circuit id, never serialised to the client", and add the
   deliberately-violating fixture that proves the rule fails.
2. **Reconnect compares the tenant but not the subject.** A reconnect that presents a valid cookie for a
   *different user of the same tenant* passes checkpoint 2 as specified, and resumes a circuit whose
   rendered state, component data and authorization context belong to the original user — horizontal
   escalation inside a tenant (ADR-0010's per-user boundary), which `CLAUDE.md` says matters as much as
   crossing tenants. Yes, it needs the circuit id; circuit ids leak through logs, crash reports, proxies
   and any XSS, and "you need one secret" is not the argument this design makes anywhere else.

**Fix:** checkpoint 2 compares **`tid` and `sub` and the security stamp** against the values pinned at
creation, and a mismatch on any of them aborts. State that persisted circuit state is server-side and
keyed to the authenticated subject. Add a B-18.6 criterion: *reconnect to a circuit with a valid cookie
for a different user in the same tenant is refused* — it fails today.

## 4. Medium

### M-1 — `403` vs `404` is a tenant-existence oracle, contradicting §4.2's own stated secret

§4.2 says *"Whether a tenant exists, and whether your session is valid for it, are both secrets from a
non-member"*, and §4/B-18.4 criterion 5 then specify `404` for "host/path maps to no tenant" and `403` for
"maps to a tenant that is not yours". Holding any valid cookie, I walk `/t/{guess}/` and read the status
code: `403` means the tenant key exists, `404` means it does not. Tenant keys are human-chosen (ADR-0007
§3.1) — company names — so this enumerates Aurora's customer list, which is commercially sensitive and is
the reconnaissance step before a targeted attack.

**Fix:** return the same status and the same Problem Details `type` for both, or `404` for both (a
non-member has no business distinguishing them). Add the criterion: *the response to an unknown tenant key
and the response to a known-but-not-mine tenant key are byte-identical apart from the correlation id*.

### M-2 — "The session is not renewed on the violating response" has no mechanism and no criterion

§4.3 requires no sliding-expiration refresh on a refusal. By default the cookie handler decides to renew
during authentication — which has already happened by the time the cross-check runs — and appends the
refreshed `Set-Cookie` when the response starts, regardless of the `403`. So the stated behaviour is the
*opposite* of what the default produces, and **no acceptance criterion in B-18.4 or B-18.5 asserts it**:
the rule will ship as prose. (`CLAUDE.md` self-check 1: the claim and the mechanism that produces it must
sit together.)

**Fix:** name the mechanism (set `ShouldRenew = false` in the cookie events when the cross-check fails, or
perform the cross-check inside `OnValidatePrincipal`), and add a B-18.4 criterion: *the `403` carries no
`Set-Cookie` header*, asserted by parsing the response.

### M-3 — "Permission absent from the catalogue → `Denied`" cannot tell a typo from an unseeded tenant

§5 row 4 is the one enumerated case where a `Denied` is manufactured from an error state, which is the
thing §5 exists to prevent. For a mistyped constant, `Denied` + `Error` log is the right call and the
reasoning holds. For a tenant whose `access` seeder failed or whose schema is behind, **every** permission
is absent, every request is `Denied`, and the operator sees a `403` storm — which §5's own operational
rule says means "a misconfigured role", when it means "this tenant is broken". The two are
indistinguishable at evaluation time with the rule as written, and they are distinguishable with one
number.

**Fix:** if the tenant's catalogue is **empty** (or its `access` schema version is behind — the §7.5 skew
gate already knows), that is **no decision** → `503`. A single absent constant in a populated catalogue
stays `Denied`. Add it to the table and to B-17.2.

### M-4 — `attempted_email_hash`: unsalted SHA-256 does not do what §7 says it does

§7 claims the hash *"does not widen ADR-0007 §9.3's single recorded personal-data exception to people who
are not users."* It does. An unsalted hash over an enumerable address space is reversible by anyone with
the table and a wordlist — a breach corpus plus commodity hardware recovers most real addresses in
minutes — and under GDPR a pseudonymised identifier is still personal data (Recital 26). So the catalog
holds recoverable email addresses of **third parties who are not Aurora users at all**, in an append-only
table, with no retention rule and no erasure path (ADR-0018's erasure covers `catalog.identity_user`, not
this). `CLAUDE.md`'s "no personal data in logs" is being satisfied by a transformation that does not
achieve it.

**Fix:** HMAC-SHA-256 with a key from the secret store (ADR-0011). Correlation across attempts is
preserved exactly; offline enumeration needs the key; rotating the key ages the correlation out by design.
Add a retention window for `catalog.authentication_event` and the platform job that enforces it, and say
in §7 that the hash is a correlation key, not an anonymisation.

### M-5 — No password policy or lockout parameters anywhere, and the framework default fails the stated standard

`CLAUDE.md` names **ASVS L2** as the reference. B-18.2 criterion 4 requires lockout "enforced through the
framework's own sign-in path" but names no thresholds; nothing anywhere names `PasswordOptions`. ASP.NET
Core Identity's defaults are 6 characters with character-class requirements — below ASVS V2.1.1's
12-character minimum, and the character-class rule is contrary to V2.1.9. A default nobody chose is a
default nobody can be shown to have chosen.

**Fix:** pin `PasswordOptions` (≥ 12 characters, no composition rules), `LockoutOptions`
(attempts, window, duration) and the invitation token TTL in ADR-0029 or B-18.2, with a test that asserts
the configured values — and a note that a breached-password check (ASVS V2.1.7) is a named follow-up
rather than an omission.

### M-6 — A denial writes an audit row, and denials are free to provoke

§7 row 6 writes one tenant-side `audit.audit_event` per enforcement-point denial. Audit writes serialise
per tenant on `pg_advisory_xact_lock` (ADR-0028 §5). Any authenticated member of a tenant can therefore
loop on a guarded endpoint they lack permission for and (a) grow the tenant's hash-chained, append-only,
partitioned table without bound, and (b) contend the per-tenant audit lock that **every business write in
that tenant** needs. That is a cheap intra-tenant denial of service by the least privileged user in the
tenant, and the customer cannot delete the rows — they are append-only by design.

**Fix:** collapse repeated identical denials per `(actor, permission, request type)` within a window into
one row with a count, or rate-limit the enforcement pipeline's audit write specifically. State the
behaviour in §7 so B-17.3 criterion 5's "exactly one row" is written against the collapsed semantics
rather than needing to change later.

### M-7 — Password hashes live in the shared catalog, reachable by the role every request uses

`catalog.identity_credential` (ADR-0029 §2) puts every platform user's password hash in the one database
every request touches for routing. Nothing in the design restricts which code, or which database role, may
read that column. ADR-0007 §3.5 stage 1 uses a single `aurora_app` login; B-05's finding M-1 already showed
this project shipping over-broad catalog grants once. Separating credentials into their own row was the
right move (§2) — finish it at the privilege level.

**Fix:** add a B-18.1 criterion: the hash column is readable only by the identity store's own role (or at
minimum, a test asserting no EF query outside `Aurora.Platform.Identity` projects it), probed as
`aurora_app` the way B-16.1/B-18.1 criterion 2 already probe append-only.

## 5. Low

- **L-1 — Exception messages.** `TenantClaimMismatchException` will carry tenant ids in its `Message` if
  written normally; §4.2's "the body names neither tenant" then depends on the Problem Details handler
  never echoing `ex.Message`, and on `DetailedErrors` staying off (ADR-0005 rule 6). Say the exception
  carries ids in structured properties only.
- **L-2 — Counter granularity.** Distinguish "no `tid`" from "`tid` mismatch" in the counter and log (not
  in the response body). Without it, H-6's fleet-wide claim-drop and a genuine replay attack are the same
  line on the dashboard, which is exactly what §4's "two different alarms" principle is trying to avoid.
- **L-3 — Invitation token in a URL** (B-18.3). A 256-bit bearer credential in a link lands in browser
  history, referrer headers and proxy logs. Deliver it in a fragment or require a POST of the token into
  the redeem form.
- **L-4 — Antiforgery token rotation at sign-in** (B-18.5 criterion 1). The auth cookie is replaced;
  say the antiforgery token is regenerated too (ASVS V3.2.1's session-fixation intent applies to both).

## 7. Route elsewhere

- **project-manager:** the glossary split (Tenant Membership vs Role Assignment) that ADR-0029 §1
  requests, and — new — the **operator capability model**. B-2 and H-3 both land on it: "who may
  provision", "who may read an invitation token" and "what an operator may do to a tenant user" are
  outside the tenant permission model (ADR-0010 rule 8) and outside ADR-0029, which means they are
  currently nobody's. SPEC-001 BR-1 assumes it exists.
- **architect:** ADR-0029 needs the amendments above before any of the nine rows is dispatched. Separately,
  two questions this review surfaced that belong in a different ADR: (a) who may write
  `catalog.tenant_host` and what verifies a custom domain — a tenant-influenced row in the only shared
  database that steers routing is a tenant→platform escalation path, and `verified_at` implies a flow
  nobody has designed; (b) what the health endpoints expose — ADR-0007 §9.4 has them enumerate "this
  instance's cached tenants", which unauthenticated is a customer list.
- **orchestrator:** B-17.1 exceeds the size rule on its own admission — split it before dispatch, not
  after. Carried forward: **B-05 finding M-1** (over-broad catalog default privileges) is being retrofitted
  per-table by B-16.1 and B-18.1 criterion 2; that is the right fix and it is now depended on by two more
  rows — do not let either drop it.

**Verdict: CHANGES_REQUESTED.** Two blockers and nine high findings, all of them design-level and all of
them cheaper to fix in this document than in B-18.5. The core judgement calls in ADR-0029 — refuse rather
than re-mint, `tid` as constraint not source, absence is mismatch, two constructible outcomes, no
permissions in the cookie, the audit split, and not reusing the `SchemaBlocked` exception — are all
correct, and the replay test is the right test. What the document is missing is the second half of its own
principle: every one of the findings above is a place where the guarantee is carried by a convention
(middleware ordering, an unnamed cache key, "the caller will pass the right scope", "the framework will
leave the principal alone") instead of by a mechanism that cannot be expressed wrongly.
